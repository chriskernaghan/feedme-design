A startup can spend weeks improving a feature that customers never needed. A shop can face the same problem after a redesign: the page looks better, but people still cannot work out whether a product suits them.

The [GOV.UK Service Standard](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/service-standard/point-1-understand-user-needs) puts understanding users at the start of service design. The principle is useful beyond government. A team needs evidence about the task before it commits to a solution.

## Our view

Understanding users helps a business make better bets. It does not guarantee growth. Revenue also depends on factors such as demand and cost, which an interface change cannot settle on its own.

Research can expose a more specific problem. Imagine shoppers leaving a product page because they cannot judge its size. A team might assume the price is too high and offer a discount. Watching people attempt the purchase could suggest a different response: clearer measurements and a useful photograph.

That is a hypothetical example, but the decision pattern is common enough to test in your own work. Analytics can show where people leave. A research session can help explain what they expected at that point.

Recruit beyond the people who already love the product. Government guidance recommends [actual or likely users](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/find-user-research-participants), including disabled participants. A convenient group of colleagues will miss some of the conditions your customers face.

## What to do about it

Choose one decision you are about to make. Write down the assumption behind it, then invite a small group of relevant users to attempt the task without a walkthrough. A few sessions can reveal problems; they cannot establish how common each problem is across your whole audience.

Use what you observe to make one change. Check the result against a measure that fits the task, such as successful completion, and keep researching as the service changes.
