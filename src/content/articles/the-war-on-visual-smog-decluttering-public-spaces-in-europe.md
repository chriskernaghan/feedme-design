Grenoble offers a concrete example in the debate over visual clutter. A [city press release](https://www.grenoble.fr/uploads/Presse/pse_fichier/88/1421_103_Avec-le-RLPi-toujours-moins-de-panneaux-publicitaires.pdf) says the end of its JCDecaux contract led to the removal of 326 advertising displays in 2015, freeing about 2,000 square metres of advertising space.

A [European circular-city catalogue](https://circulareconomy.europa.eu/platform/sites/default/files/2025-02/a-catalogue-of-circular-city-actions-and-solutions.pdf) traces the contract decision to 2014. These dates describe different stages of the same change. They do not establish that all advertising disappeared from Grenoble or that Europe followed a single policy.

## Our view

Visual smog is a useful phrase for a design problem: many separate messages can compete for attention in one shared place. Each may make sense to the person who added it. The combined experience can still be tiring.

A street and a shop website have different responsibilities, but there is a useful comparison. A product page may accumulate a discount banner, a chat prompt and an email sign-up overlay. The team responsible for each addition can explain its purpose. The shopper encounters the whole page at once.

Removing an element can therefore be a design decision in its own right. It can give the remaining information room to work.

That is an interpretation of the case, not evidence that fewer messages always improve sales. Advertising can fund services, and removing it can involve real trade-offs. A digital team should measure the effects of its own choices.

## What to do about it

Open your shop on a phone as a first-time visitor. Count how many prompts appear before you can assess one product.

Choose one low-value interruption to remove or delay. Check whether people can complete the task more easily and whether the change affects the business measure that prompt was meant to support.
