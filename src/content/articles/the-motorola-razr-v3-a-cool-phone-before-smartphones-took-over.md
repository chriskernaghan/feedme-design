The Motorola Razr V3 arrived in 2004. Its thin clamshell body and metal keypad gave it a character that remains easy to recognise. [iFixit’s device record](https://www.ifixit.com/Device/Motorola_RAZR_v3) places the original model’s release in the third quarter of that year.

Motorola’s [product sheet for the black edition](https://www.ifixit.com/Document/JgKrBPLWkQYtYvBX/Motorola-Special-Edition-Black-RAZR-V3-Product-Sheet.pdf) puts the physical design at the centre of its pitch. That focus helps explain why the phone is still worth discussing as an object.

## Our view

The V3 had a clear design idea. Its narrow profile was apparent before anyone opened a menu. Opening the hinge revealed a keypad that belonged to the same visual language as the shell.

That coherence matters. A feature can sound impressive on a product page yet make little difference to the experience of ownership. The Razr’s appearance was part of every use. Taking it out of a pocket was enough to encounter the design.

There is a limit to the lesson. A distinctive shell does not establish that the menus were easy to use, and nostalgia is a poor substitute for a usability test. We should be able to admire the object without declaring every part of it successful.

For a shop, the useful question is what gives a product its identity before a customer reads the specifications. A well-chosen image can make that quality clear. A busy page can bury it beneath claims that apply to every competitor.

## What to do about it

Choose one product page and identify its strongest visible difference. Show that difference in the lead photograph, with enough context to understand its scale or use.

Then ask someone unfamiliar with the product what they remember after closing the page. Treat the answer as a clue for the next design revision, rather than proof of likely sales.
