When Nationwide announced its new identity in October 2023, the work extended beyond a replacement logo. Its announcement described changes across branches, payment cards, and customer communications as part of its largest image overhaul since 1987.

For an online shop, that breadth is the useful part of the story. A new homepage may be the most visible work, but the customer can encounter the business again through a payment screen or an order update that follows a different design system.

A debate about whether the new logo looks better will not tell you whether those parts still belong together.

## Our view

A rebrand should preserve a clear sense of who the customer is dealing with throughout a purchase.

This is our application of the Nationwide example to retail, rather than a claim about the effect of its rebrand on customer behaviour. Its announcement describes the intended work; it does not establish that the new identity increased trust or improved a particular task.

For a shop, consider a hypothetical customer arriving through an older promotional email. They follow the link to a redesigned store, add an item to their basket, and reach a hosted payment page showing the previous logo. The receipt then comes from a sender name they have never seen before.

Each part may function as intended. Taken together, however, the journey gives the customer several changes to interpret while they are deciding whether to pay. It is sensible to inspect those changes before declaring the rebrand complete.

Consistency does not require every screen to look identical. A payment provider may control much of its own interface, and a delivery company will use its own identity. The shop still needs to explain those handovers and use the branding controls available to it.

The important distinction is between a change the customer can understand and one the team has simply forgotten to review.

A visual update can also become tangled with changes to how the shop works. Moving the account link or renaming product categories may seem like part of making a cleaner design, but each creates another question for returning customers. A successful logo review says little about whether those changes help people find an earlier order.

Treat those decisions as separate work that needs checking. Keep a familiar label if it still describes the task, even when the typography around it changes. Where navigation does need to move, test the new arrangement with people who know the old shop as well as people arriving for the first time.

There is also a practical limit to what a rebrand can achieve. A more assured visual identity will not explain a missing refund or make an unclear delivery promise accurate. The design should express the business customers will encounter when something goes wrong, rather than offering a polished version of a service the shop cannot provide.

That makes the rollout a useful opportunity to review the purchase journey. It should not become an excuse to postpone ordinary fixes until the new colours are ready.

## What to do about it

### Follow one order beyond the new homepage

Run a test purchase through your normal payment setup, using a test environment where available. Start from an existing campaign or product link so the review includes an entry point outside the redesigned site.

Capture the changes in identity you encounter through to the receipt. Include a return or support request if those flows are available to test. Record which system owns each screen or message, since the person editing the theme may have no control over the email template.

For each mismatch, decide whether to update it, explain it, or accept it as a clear handover to another service. Assign the unresolved items before launch.

### Check recognition with a returning customer

Show someone familiar with the shop the proposed journey and ask them to place a test order. Avoid introducing it as a brand review, which may turn the conversation into a discussion of personal taste.

Ask what business they believe they are paying when they reach the payment screen. Afterwards, ask where they would go to find the order or request help. If their answer changes at an unexpected point, inspect what on the screen led them there.

This is a small comprehension check, not proof that the rebrand will improve sales. Its value is finding a confusing handover while it is still straightforward to fix.

A rebrand is ready when the customer can get on with buying. The launch presentation is only one place where it needs to hold together.
