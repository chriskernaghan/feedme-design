Delivery costs are one of those things every shop has an opinion about, and most shoppers only notice when they can't find them. We've been through a couple of real baskets on feedme.design, plus Baymard's research on product pages, and the same handful of patterns keep turning up.

A quick note on the examples. The MUJI and John Lewis observations come from single desktop sessions on 14 November 2026, with one item in the basket and no order placed, so they show what appeared in that session and nothing more. Both shops may well have changed things since, and a different basket or address could give a different answer. They're here because they illustrate the patterns clearly, and neither one is the worst offender by a long way.

## 1. The cost only turns up at checkout

"Delivery calculated at checkout" hands the shopper a job before they know what the purchase costs. Baymard reports that 67% of the sites it assessed miss a total order cost estimate near the buy section, although its public summary doesn't give the exact base for that figure.

**Instead:** put a delivery charge or an estimate beside the buying controls. We covered it in [delivery costs are still missing from too many product pages](/articles/missing-delivery-costs/).

## 2. The estimate is there, but it's below the fold

When we looked at MUJI's product page, a "Standard UK Delivery from £3.95" block sat below the purchase controls, and in our initial desktop view it was off the bottom of the screen. It was useful information, but you had to go and find it.

**Instead:** check where your delivery message lands on a small phone with the cookie banner showing, because that's the screen most people are actually using.

## 3. The calculator is behind a vague label

MUJI's basket let us get a delivery quote without entering an email address, which is good. The control for it said "Add Info", which doesn't tell you what to add or what you'll get back.

**Instead:** name it after the result. "Estimate delivery" is our suggestion to test, and it's a proposal rather than a proven improvement. The full walkthrough is in [breaking down MUJI's checkout](/articles/breaking-down-muji-delivery-costs/).

## 4. The free delivery message answers a different question

With one £12.95 diary in the basket, MUJI told us we were £72.05 away from free standard shipping. That's helpful if you're planning to spend £85, but someone buying just the diary wants to know what delivery will cost for that. In our session it was £3.95, which is about 30.5% of the diary's price, and that's arithmetic from this basket rather than an abandonment statistic.

**Instead:** show the current cost of delivery beside the threshold message, so both questions are answered together.

## 5. The price shown on the product page vanishes from the basket

John Lewis's product page told us delivery was free over £50, otherwise from £4.50, and the delivery drawer listed standard delivery at £4.50. In the basket, the summary told us to spend another £10 for free delivery and marked the £40 total as excluding delivery, but the £4.50 wasn't repeated. Someone who missed the drawer had to go back to find it. There's more in [breaking down the John Lewis basket](/articles/john-lewis-basket-delivery-costs/).

**Instead:** repeat the known charge beside the basket total, and say what's still uncertain.

## 6. "Subtotal" keeps changing its meaning

At MUJI, the basket's "Subtotal" was £12.95 before we asked for a delivery quote and £16.90 after it, and then checkout used the same word for the £12.95 of goods again. None of it is wrong, but you end up reconciling the numbers yourself.

**Instead:** show items, delivery and an estimated total as separate lines, and keep the labels the same from the basket onwards.

## 7. The estimate doesn't say where it's for

Delivery prices usually depend on the destination, and a number with no destination attached is hard to trust. Baymard's examples show an editable location sitting next to the estimate.

**Instead:** show the postcode or country the estimate assumes, with a control to change it. If you can't quote yet, say what you need, and don't show the cheapest possible rate as if it applied everywhere.

## 8. Heavy items get the standard parcel message

MUJI's shipping panel listed separate furniture charges alongside the parcel bands, and Baymard's examples include shops where a surcharge changes the delivered price. A sofa shouldn't carry a message written for a diary.

**Instead:** test one item with special rules and check that the product page quotes the real charge, or explains what information it needs to work it out.

## Where to start

If you only have time to check one, take a product below your free delivery threshold, open it signed out on your phone, and see whether you can work out the delivered price from the page. If you can't, number 1 is the place to begin, and the how-to is in [how to show delivery costs before customers reach checkout](/articles/how-to-show-delivery-costs/).

None of this proves that changing it will lift sales. That needs a proper experiment with enough traffic. It does tell you whether a shopper can answer the simplest question they have, which is how much it'll cost to get the thing to their door.
