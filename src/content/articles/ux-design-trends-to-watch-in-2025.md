> **Archive note:** This is a newly researched replacement for an unavailable Feed Me Design article. It was prepared on 13 November 2026 and is not a recovered copy of the original.

A page about trends to watch in 2025 now needs a retrospective frame. The year has passed, and the original forecast is not available to check. This replacement examines how to judge an emerging interface idea without pretending to score predictions we cannot recover.

Immersive experiences provide a useful example. The [W3C WebXR specification](https://www.w3.org/TR/webxr/) describes interfaces for building virtual and augmented reality experiences on the web. That technical possibility does not establish demand for a particular shopping feature.

## Our view

A trend is a prompt for a question. It should not become a requirement before the team has found a problem worth solving.

An augmented-reality view might help a shopper judge how a large object fits into a room. It might add little when someone needs to check a basic measurement. These are hypotheses to test with the product and audience in question.

The rest of the purchase still matters. If delivery terms remain unclear, a compelling preview has not resolved the whole task. A customer also needs a useful route when their device cannot support the immersive feature or they choose not to use it.

The [GOV.UK principle of understanding user needs](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/service-standard/point-1-understand-user-needs) provides a firmer starting point than a trend list. Establish the uncertainty that stops a person making progress. Then assess whether the new interaction can reduce it.

This approach leaves room for experiments. It also gives a team a reason to stop one when the evidence is weak.

## What to do about it

For any trend you are considering, write a short hypothesis naming the user’s problem and the change you expect to see. Build the smallest version that can test it.

Compare it with a simpler option, such as a better photograph or clearer instructions. Keep the feature only if the improvement justifies the work required to maintain it.
