You don't need a research budget to find out where your checkout goes wrong. You need a phone, a fresh browser session where you're signed out, a test order, and ideally someone who has never used your shop. Give them a simple task, such as buying one thing, don't point anything out, and write down every spot where they hesitate.

These are the seven things we'd look for first. Each one comes from research we've already reviewed on feedme.design, and the figures are Baymard's, not ours, so they tell you what to look at rather than what will happen in your shop.

## 1. The guest route is hard to find

If someone has to work out how to avoid making an account, some of them will make one, and some of them will leave. Baymard's examples show that indirect wording leaves people unsure whether registration is compulsory.

**Spot it:** open checkout signed out and see how long it takes to find the guest option. **Fix it:** use a prominent button that says "Continue as guest" near the top of the account choice. We've written the full version in [how to make guest checkout easy to find](/articles/how-to-improve-guest-checkout/).

## 2. The account invitation turns up too early

Baymard reports that 56% of the sites it assessed don't leave account creation until the order is confirmed, although its public summary doesn't give the exact base. It also observed that early invitations interrupted people even when registration was optional. There's more in [let customers finish buying before asking them to join](/articles/account-creation-checkout/).

**Spot it:** go through as a guest and note every point where the word "account" or a password field appears. **Fix it:** move the optional invitation to the confirmation page, and only promise a benefit your shop really offers.

## 3. Fields that don't say whether you can skip them

Baymard's field-label study reports missed required fields among 32% of participants who faced forms marking only the optional ones. That's a testing result rather than a forecast, but it's a good reason to be explicit.

**Spot it:** on your phone, scroll until the page heading is out of view, then read the next field and see whether you can tell if it's needed. **Fix it:** label both required and optional fields where they appear, and check that blank fields behave the way the labels say. Our guide to [clearer mobile checkout fields](/articles/how-to-clarify-mobile-checkout-fields/) goes through it.

## 4. A phone number with no explanation

Baymard's 2026 checkout review describes participants giving false numbers, or abandoning, when a phone field came with no explanation.

**Spot it:** look for a required phone field and ask whether the page says why. **Fix it:** ask whoever handles fulfilment how the number is used, and say so beside the field. If nobody can name a use, consider removing the field altogether.

## 5. Delivery costs that only appear at checkout

Baymard reports that 67% of the sites it assessed miss a total order cost estimate near the buy section, again without disclosing the exact base. We looked at it in [delivery costs are still missing from too many product pages](/articles/missing-delivery-costs/).

**Spot it:** open a product page signed out and try to work out what you'd pay delivered. **Fix it:** show the delivery charge or an estimate beside the buying controls, for example "£27.95 including standard delivery", which is an illustration and not anyone's real price.

## 6. A free delivery threshold with no mention of the normal charge

"Free delivery over £50" leaves a shopper with a £47 basket guessing. They need to see what delivery costs below the line to decide whether adding another item is worth it.

**Spot it:** pick an item below your threshold and see whether the normal charge is visible. **Fix it:** show both, such as "Standard delivery £3.95. Free on eligible orders of £50 or more", using your own terms.

## 7. An estimate that doesn't match the basket

A clear estimate that turns out to be wrong is worse than no estimate, because the shopper trusted it.

**Spot it:** note the delivery quote on a product page, add the item to the basket, enter the same address at checkout without paying, and compare the two. **Fix it:** if the amounts differ, look at your delivery rules before you touch any wording. We've set out the whole check in [how to show delivery costs before customers reach checkout](/articles/how-to-show-delivery-costs/).

## What the test can and can't tell you

Five minutes with a phone will show you where people hesitate and where your pages contradict each other. It won't prove that fixing something lifts sales, because that takes a controlled experiment with enough traffic.

If you only have time for one fix this week, make the guest route obvious. It's the cheapest change on the list, and every other problem matters less to someone who has already left.
