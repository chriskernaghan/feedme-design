The teal and purple strokes of the Jazz cup became recognisable far beyond the disposable tableware they decorated. In 2015, reporting brought attention to Gina Ekiss, the Sweetheart designer credited with creating the pattern, after an online search for the person behind it.

The attribution needs a qualification. In later reporting, 99% Invisible covered a competing claim from designer Stephanie Miller and reported that the company continued to credit Ekiss. That account does not give us grounds to resolve the dispute ourselves.

For an online shop, the useful question raised by Jazz is how much work a product’s appearance can do before someone reads its name. Packaging may be distinctive in the hand, yet barely visible in the image a customer first encounters.

## Our view

Treat product photography as part of the packaging design’s job.

The Jazz pattern gives us a specific object to consider. Broad coloured strokes occupy a mostly white surface, with enough visual character to make an ordinary cup identifiable. That is a visual observation, not evidence that a similar treatment would increase sales in another category.

Nor can its later popularity be turned into a reliable formula. We do not have a comparison showing what would have happened with a different pattern. The sensible lesson for a retailer is to examine what remains visible when a designed object becomes a small image on a screen.

Imagine a shop selling tea in illustrated tins. The packaging designer has given each blend a distinctive drawing, but the collection page uses distant photographs with the lids facing the camera. The work that distinguishes one tin from another has disappeared before the customer reaches a product page.

That is an illustrative problem, but it is straightforward to check. View the real listing at its normal size and ask what a customer can use to tell the products apart. Enlarging the photograph in an editing app answers a different question.

A more recognisable image still needs to help someone select the right item. A returning customer may remember the blue tin, but the shop should also show the blend name and pack size. Colour can support the choice without becoming the only way to make it.

This matters when a packaging range expands. An approach that separates three products may become harder to follow when several variants share similar colours. Review the whole collection together, rather than signing off each new pack in isolation.

The product page can then show the details that a thumbnail cannot carry. A close view might establish the finish, while a separate image of the label helps someone inspect the contents. Choose those views around the questions relevant to the product instead of filling a gallery with near-identical angles.

The maker’s story has a place here too. The public interest in the person behind Jazz is a useful reminder to credit creative work accurately. For a retailer commissioning packaging, a concise account of who designed it can give interested customers somewhere to go beyond the product specifications.

That story should be based on records you can stand behind. A memorable origin tale is not a reason to turn an uncertain attribution into a settled fact, or to imply that familiarity with a design gives the shop permission to reproduce it.

## What to do about it

### Review one collection at thumbnail size

Choose a collection with related products and open it on a phone. Look at the default images without zooming. Ask someone unfamiliar with the range to find a named variant and explain how they identified it.

If the useful part of the packaging is hidden, try a different camera angle or crop on a small set of images. Keep the comparison fair by showing the versions at the same display size. Check that the new crop still represents the amount and format being sold.

For returning customers, try a second task based on a previous purchase. Their memory of the pack may reveal a useful cue that the existing photograph has lost.

### Connect the image with a clear product label

Check that the name beside each thumbnail identifies the variant and that the selected product page shows the same one. Follow it into the basket, where a different image or abbreviated label can introduce fresh uncertainty.

Record any points where the participant selects the wrong item or needs to open several pages to distinguish them. Use those observations to revise the images and labels together.

This is a practical design check, not a measured claim about conversion. The goal is to find out whether the packaging’s distinctive work remains useful when the customer encounters it through your shop.
