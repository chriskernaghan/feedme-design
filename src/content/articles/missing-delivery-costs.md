The price beside a product should help someone decide whether they can afford it. A delivery charge that appears later can change that decision.

For a shop owner, this raises a useful question. Can someone work out what they will pay before adding an item to their basket?

## How we measured it

This is a review of [Baymard’s published benchmark](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-ecommerce-product-page-ux), rather than an original audit. We extracted its finding on total order cost estimates. We did not test individual shops or recalculate its percentage.

The public summary does not provide enough underlying records to reproduce the result independently. Its wider sample should not be treated as the denominator for this specific measure.

## Results

| Measure | Reported share |
| --- | --- |
| Sites missing a total order cost estimate near the buy section | 67% |

Source: Baymard Institute, March 2026 update.

This measure covers the order estimate, not just whether a delivery policy exists somewhere on the website.

## What to check on your own shop

Start with a product someone might buy on its own. Open the page on your phone while signed out and try to work out the delivered price. Record where you find each part of the answer. If you need the basket, note that too.

For example, imagine a £24 item with £3.95 delivery. A useful piece of copy to test would be: “£27.95 including standard UK delivery.” That example is illustrative, not a finding from the benchmark.

If delivery depends on the address, test whether someone can get a quote using only their postcode. Check the wording with an address outside your usual delivery area. An estimate that works for Belfast but changes for another destination needs a clear explanation of its scope.

Free delivery thresholds deserve their own check. Take an item below the threshold and see whether the page explains the charge for buying it alone. A banner advertising free delivery over £50 leaves that question open.

Before changing the design, capture the existing page. Ask a few customers to tell you what they expect to pay, then repeat the task with your proposed version. Their answers can help you spot wording that your team has learned to overlook.

## Limits

This is not a UK market estimate or a count of shops audited by feedme.design. It does not establish a conversion uplift from changing delivery information.

The source is a dated benchmark summary. Individual shops may have changed since assessment. Treat the finding as a reason to inspect your own buying journey, rather than a prediction of how your customers will behave.
