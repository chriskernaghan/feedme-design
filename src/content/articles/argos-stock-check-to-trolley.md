A product being available somewhere doesn't tell a shopper whether they can collect it from a useful location. Argos addresses that question on the product page, where its stock checker leads into a list of stores with collection timings.

We followed that route with a white Sony DualSense PS5 controller priced at £59.99. The selected store carried through to the trolley, but the journey between those screens introduced an insurance offer and an accessories panel. Those stages became the focus of this review: how well does a shop preserve the shopper's chosen purchase while offering something else?

**Method and scope.** We inspected the UK Argos website on 15 November 2026 in desktop Chrome, using a viewport of approximately 1,360 × 930 pixels. We searched for a PS5 controller, opened product 834/9103 and entered “Belfast” in the stock checker. We selected Belfast Castle Court Argos and added one controller without extras. Required cookies only were accepted. This was a single expert walkthrough, with no participant testing or conversion data. It wasn't a mobile test.

The review ends at the trolley. Selecting Pay now and collect triggered a human-verification check in this research browser, so we didn't inspect the account, customer-details or payment screens. No purchase was made. Availability and prices below describe the captured session.

## What the flow does

### 1. The product page establishes the item before asking for a location

The page showed the controller's name, colour and £59.99 price, with the stock checker further down the purchase column. Its introductory text explained that checking stock would reveal delivery and collection options. Add to trolley sat below the checker.

That explanation gives the location field a purpose. The shopper can understand what the information will unlock before supplying it. In our desktop view, however, the stock section began near the bottom of the first screen, below credit options, loyalty information and bundle links.

### 2. A town search produces collection choices

Entering “Belfast” opened a Find a collection store panel. Each result combined a store name with its address and collection timing. The panel also offered a map and opening-times link, alongside a reminder that collection orders need to be paid for online.

Belfast Castle Court appeared first, with a message saying the item could be collected right away. Other results had different timings, including collection from 10am the following day. We didn't independently verify physical stock or opening hours, so these should be read as the interface's availability messages.

The result card offered both Select this store and Add to trolley. We chose Select this store, which let us review the resulting product-page state before adding the item.

![Collection-store panel showing Belfast Castle Court, a collection availability message, its address and controls for selecting the store or adding the item.](/images/breakdowns/argos-collection/02-store-choices.jpg)

*Captured 15 November 2026. The search used a town name, not a personal address. Distances are those shown by Argos for that search.*

### 3. The product page confirms the chosen store

After selection, the purchase area showed Collection as the selected option and named Belfast Castle Court. A Change store control remained beside it, making the choice reversible without starting again.

The same section also contained an Argos Plus home-delivery promotion above the collection and delivery controls. That promotion wasn't added to the trolley, but it occupied space within the area used to explain how this item would be obtained.

![Product-page collection state naming Belfast Castle Court, with Change store and Add to trolley controls beneath a home-delivery pass promotion.](/images/breakdowns/argos-collection/03-store-selected.jpg)

*Captured 15 November 2026. Collection is visibly selected and the store name is repeated before Add to trolley.*

### 4. Adding the controller opens an optional-cover panel

The interface confirmed that the item had been added, then used the side panel to present replacement cover. A two-year option was shown at £16.99, with information about the cover and links to its documents.

Continue without insurance was visible at the bottom of the captured panel. We used it and didn't add cover. The critique here concerns the placement of this decision within the purchase journey; it isn't an assessment of the policy or its value.

![Argos side panel confirming the item was added, presenting optional replacement cover and showing Continue without insurance.](/images/breakdowns/argos-collection/04-optional-cover.jpg)

*Captured 15 November 2026. The panel provides an explicit route to proceed without cover. Promotional claims visible in the screenshot weren't independently assessed.*

### 5. Declining cover leads to accessories

The next panel offered charging-station bundles and recommended extras. Go to trolley remained visible in its bottom action area, so the shopper could proceed without adding another product.

The panel's heading named the controller, preserving the product context. The selected collection store wasn't repeated in either of the captured offer panels, although it had been prominent on the product page.

![Accessories panel showing charging-station offers and recommended extras, with Go to trolley visible at the bottom.](/images/breakdowns/argos-collection/05-extras.jpg)

*Captured 15 November 2026. We selected Go to trolley without adding a bundle or an accessory.*

### 6. The trolley retains the collection choice

The trolley contained one controller at £59.99 and named Belfast Castle Court in its collection column. It repeated the collection-availability message and offered a way to choose another store. The order summary listed collection as free.

Home delivery remained a separate route requiring a postcode check. That distinction matters: choosing a collection store didn't establish delivery availability for a home address.

Further down the page, Pay now and collect was the available continuation control, while Continue with delivery was disabled. We selected the collection control and reached the verification check described in the method note. We can't draw conclusions about the checkout beyond that point.

## What works

The stock results turn a broad location search into a practical choice. A store name alone would leave the shopper to investigate whether that branch could fulfil the order; the collection timing gives them information needed to judge the option. The online-payment reminder also helps explain what collection involves before checkout.

The selected store remains visible after the modal closes and carries into the trolley. This preserves a decision the shopper has already made. Change store stays available, allowing correction without removing the controller or repeating the product search.

The optional-offer panels provide clear ways to proceed. We could decline cover and move past accessories without adding either. These are useful controls, even though the placement of the offers creates a separate design question.

## What costs sales

We haven't measured lost sales. The concerns below are design judgements to test, rather than claims about Argos's conversion rate.

Once a shopper has chosen where to collect, the next two panels shift attention to optional purchases. In our route, continuing without cover was followed by another offer stage before the trolley. The product remained identified, but the chosen store wasn't repeated within those panels.

Our concern is continuity. A shopper checking whether they can collect a controller soon has resolved an important constraint, and that resolution should remain easy to see while the interface introduces other decisions. A compact confirmation containing the item and chosen store could preserve that context without removing the offers.

The home-delivery pass above the selected collection controls is another candidate for testing. It may be useful to someone considering delivery, but its relevance is less clear once collection has been chosen. We would test moving it into the delivery route and observe whether shoppers can identify their selected fulfilment method more easily.

Neither proposal establishes that the existing design is commercially ineffective. Optional offers may generate value, and this walkthrough doesn't measure that benefit. The question is whether the same opportunities can be presented with less interruption to the configured purchase.

## What to take from it

Review one collection journey on your own shop, starting with an item available at more than one branch. After selecting a store, check whether its name remains visible through any offers and into the basket. Where it disappears, consider adding a short purchase summary beside the continuation control.

For a focused experiment, compare the current offer sequence with a version that provides a direct route to the trolley while keeping extras accessible. Measure completed orders as well as attachment of extras, and ask participants to identify their collection store before proceeding. That will help distinguish a successful purchase from an increase in optional-product clicks.
