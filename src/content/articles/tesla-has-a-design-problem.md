Tesla’s approach to controls raises a design question: what happens when a sparse cabin requires people to learn more about the interface?

The details depend on the vehicle and software. The [European Model 3 manual for 2024+ cars](https://www.tesla.com/ownersmanual/model3/en_gb/) documents several ways to operate the wipers in its [Wipers and Washers section](https://www.tesla.com/ownersmanual/model3/en_gb/Owners_Manual.pdf). These include a steering-wheel button, scroll-wheel adjustment and touchscreen settings. It would be wrong to claim that every wiper action requires a touchscreen.

## Our view

The useful criticism concerns the effort required to find and operate a control under the conditions of use. Counting the buttons on a dashboard cannot answer that question.

A control may be discoverable during a calm demonstration and harder to understand when someone needs it at once. Its position, feedback and relationship to other controls all matter. Familiarity can help an owner, but a new driver still needs a workable first experience.

This is a design argument rather than a finding about crashes. The manual establishes how the documented controls work. It does not show how often a particular interaction causes an error or an accident, and this article makes no such claim.

The same question is useful on a shop website, where the stakes differ. Hiding delivery details behind an unfamiliar icon may make a layout look cleaner. It can also make a routine purchase harder to understand.

A good review should examine the task in context before deciding whether visual simplicity has improved the experience.

## What to do about it

Choose a frequent action in your own product and ask a new user to find it without help. Watch where they look before giving any hints.

If the action is hidden, try a visible text label or a more direct route. Compare the attempts before making the change permanent.
