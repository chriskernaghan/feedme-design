Most of the checkout testing advice on the internet starts with a tool, and fair enough, we wrote [a piece on four of them](/articles/four-tools-for-testing-your-checkout/) ourselves. But the tools mostly tell you where people leave. They're much less useful for telling you why, and the why is usually sitting in plain view if you go and look. Here's the free version, which takes about an hour and a phone.

## Place three real orders yourself

Not test orders on a staging site with a discount code and a saved address. Real ones, as a customer who has never been to your shop. Use a card that isn't saved anywhere, a guest checkout, and a delivery address you have to type out in full.

Do one on your phone, on mobile data rather than wifi, because that's where most of your buyers are and where most of the friction hides. Do one on a laptop. Do one where you deliberately make a mistake, like a mistyped postcode or an expired card, and see what the site says back.

Write down every moment you hesitate, even for a second. A hesitation is a drop-off that happened to be you, and you're stubborn enough to carry on.

## Watch one stranger do it

This is the bit people skip, and it's the bit that matters. Ask someone who has never used your shop to buy a specific thing, give them a phone, and then say nothing. Don't point, don't explain, don't wince.

Ask them to talk through what they're thinking as they go. The useful lines tend to be small ones, like "where do I put the discount code" or "hang on, is that the total or just the items". Each of those is a screen or a label that isn't doing its job.

Three people is plenty. By the second one you'll start seeing the same stumbles repeat, and that's the signal that the problem is the checkout and not the person.

## What to write down

Keep it plain. For each hesitation, note the screen, what they said or did, and what they expected to happen instead. You'll end up with a list that looks a bit embarrassing, which is how you know it's honest.

A few things worth checking on every run:

- Whether the delivery cost and delivery date are visible before the last step
- Whether the form asks for anything you don't strictly need
- What happens when the keyboard opens on a phone, and whether the button you need is still on screen
- How long the wait is after pressing pay, and whether anything tells you it's working
- Whether the error messages say what to fix, or just that something is wrong

## Then fix the loudest three

Resist the urge to fix everything. Pick the three problems that came up most or hurt most, change them, and run the same test again with fresh people. If you're checking against a list, [seven checkout mistakes you can spot in five minutes](/articles/seven-checkout-mistakes-you-can-spot-in-five-minutes/) is a decent place to compare notes.

## Where the tools come in

Once you've done this, tools become much more useful, because you know what to look for. A session recording is far easier to read when you already suspect the delivery step, and a form analytics report means more when you've watched someone get stuck on the address field. The manual test tells you what to investigate, and the tools tell you how often it happens.

If you only have time for one of them, though, do the stranger test first, since it costs nothing and you can run it this week.
