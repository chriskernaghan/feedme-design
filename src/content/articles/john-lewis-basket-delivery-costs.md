John Lewis answers a useful question before you add this cushion to your basket: what will delivery cost? Its product page gives a starting price beside the purchase controls. Open the delivery drawer and the standard charge is clear.

The basket changes the emphasis. It tells you how much more to spend to qualify for free delivery. The charge for delivering the item already in your basket is no longer shown in the summary.

That small shift is worth examining. A shopper deciding whether to buy one thing needs to understand the cost of that purchase.

**Test details:** We reviewed the UK website on 14 November 2026 in desktop Chrome, with captures around 1,360 × 930 pixels. We selected one John Lewis Wave Fur Cushion in Cocoa, priced at £40, and followed the flow from the product page to the checkout entry screen. This was one browser session. We stopped before selecting a checkout route or entering personal details; delivery selection and payment were outside the review. Prices and screens may change.

## What the flow does

### 1. The product page puts delivery near the buying decision

The [cushion page](https://www.johnlewis.com/john-lewis-wave-fur-cushion/cocoa/p115311055) showed the £40 price, stock status and an Add to basket button. Beneath the purchase controls, a short delivery summary stated that standard delivery was free over £50, otherwise from £4.50. Free Click & Collect was also shown.

This gives the shopper a useful starting point without making them open a help page. The word “from” leaves room for the specific service and destination to affect the final charge.

![John Lewis cushion product page showing the £40 price and delivery information near the purchase controls.](/images/breakdowns/john-lewis-delivery/01-product.jpg)

*Captured 14 November 2026. Delivery information appears on the product page before the shopper commits to the basket.*

### 2. A drawer gives the standard service a price and timeframe

Opening Delivery and returns revealed a side panel. Standard delivery was listed at £4.50, with a UK delivery window of two to five days. The panel also listed collection and faster delivery services.

The drawer keeps the product in view while the shopper checks a practical detail. It supports a quick detour, then a return to the buying decision.

![Delivery drawer listing standard delivery at £4.50 and a two-to-five-day timeframe.](/images/breakdowns/john-lewis-delivery/02-delivery-options.jpg)

*Captured 14 November 2026. These are the product page's published options; we did not confirm availability for a delivery address.*

### 3. Adding the item produces a clear confirmation

After we added one cushion, an inline panel named the product and quantity. A Go to your basket button appeared inside it. The delivery summary remained beneath the purchase area.

Naming the variant matters. “Cocoa” gives the shopper a chance to catch a wrong colour before moving on.

![Inline confirmation naming one Cocoa cushion with a Go to your basket button.](/images/breakdowns/john-lewis-delivery/03-added-to-basket.jpg)

*Captured 14 November 2026. The confirmation identifies what was added and provides a direct next step.*

### 4. The basket leads with the free-delivery threshold

In the [basket](https://www.johnlewis.com/basket), the cushion appeared at £40 with a quantity control. The summary told us to spend another £10 to qualify for free standard UK mainland delivery. It confirmed that this basket qualified for free Click & Collect.

The displayed total was £40, explicitly marked as excluding delivery. A line said that more delivery options would be available at checkout. The £4.50 standard charge from the product drawer was absent from this summary.

![John Lewis basket showing a £40 total excluding delivery, free collection and a prompt to spend another £10 for free standard delivery.](/images/breakdowns/john-lewis-delivery/04-basket.jpg)

*Captured 14 November 2026. The summary explains the threshold but does not repeat the standard delivery charge.*

### 5. Checkout offers a visible guest route

Continue to checkout initially produced a connection error. One refresh recovered the flow and brought up a choice between signing in and checking out as a guest. We cannot establish the cause or frequency of that error from this session.

Both routes appeared as large, labelled options. The guest option explained that an account could be created after the order. We stopped here, so this observation confirms the option's visibility, not the steps that follow it.

![Checkout entry with separate Sign in and Guest checkout options.](/images/breakdowns/john-lewis-delivery/05-checkout-entry.jpg)

*Captured 14 November 2026. Guest checkout is visible at entry. No account or payment details were entered.*

## What works

The product page gives delivery information a place near the buying controls. That helps a shopper judge the purchase before adding it to the basket. The drawer supplies more detail without sending them away from the product.

The basket also distinguishes the item total from delivery. Its label is explicit, so £40 is not presented as a confirmed delivered price. Free collection is tied to the current basket, which gives a shopper who can collect a useful answer.

At checkout entry, the guest route receives enough space to be easy to spot. The explanation about creating an account later addresses a likely question without requiring a registration decision first.

## What costs sales

We have no conversion data for John Lewis. The concern below is our design judgement, not a measured loss of sales.

The basket makes the incentive to spend more easier to see than the cost of proceeding with the current purchase. Someone who remembers the product drawer can compare the options. Someone who missed it has to continue or go back to find that price.

For this example, £40 plus the published £4.50 standard charge comes to £44.50. Adding £10 of goods to reach the basket's stated free-delivery threshold would bring spending to £50. That is £5.50 more than the illustrative delivered total, though the shopper would receive extra goods. Neither route is inherently better; the shopper needs the figures to choose.

That calculation is an illustration based on the product page. It is not a final delivery quote, because we did not enter an address or select a service.

Our proposed change is small: repeat the known delivery information beside the basket total, with any uncertainty stated there. Keep the free-delivery offer available, but let the current purchase make sense on its own.

## What to take from it

Review one basket below your free-delivery threshold this week. Check whether a shopper can work out the likely delivered cost from that screen. If an address is needed, explain that beside the total and provide a way to get the quote.

Then test a version that puts the current delivery cost next to the threshold offer. Measure completed purchases alongside order value: a larger basket alone would not show whether the change helped people buy. Keep the clear guest option when reviewing the next step.
