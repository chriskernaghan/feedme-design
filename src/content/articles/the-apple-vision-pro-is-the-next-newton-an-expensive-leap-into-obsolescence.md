Apple introduced Vision Pro in June 2023 with a three-dimensional interface controlled through eyes, hands, and voice. It was an invitation to rethink what a screen could be, which also makes it tempting to rethink what an online shop could look like.

A showroom that surrounds the customer sounds more interesting than another grid of product photographs. For a retailer considering the work involved, though, the useful question is what someone can decide in that showroom that they could not decide on the existing product page.

The old address of this article calls Vision Pro the next Newton. That prediction is more confident than the argument needs to be. A shop owner does not need to settle the future of a computing platform before deciding where to spend the next design budget.

## Our view

An immersive shopping experience should earn its place by resolving a buying question.

Consider someone choosing an armchair for a small living room. A well-made spatial preview might help them judge whether it crowds the space beside a sofa. That is a plausible use worth testing, provided the model represents the actual dimensions and the customer understands what the preview can tell them.

It would not tell them how firm the cushion feels. Nor would it explain whether delivery includes carrying the chair upstairs. Those questions still belong in the shopping journey, however impressive the rendering looks.

This is where an immersive project can become difficult to justify. The team may spend months making a product feel present while leaving its specifications incomplete. A customer can inspect the chair from every angle and still have no idea whether it arrives assembled.

Our editorial view is that the unresolved question should determine the design work. If customers are unsure about size, compare a spatial preview with a clear dimensions image or a photograph in a furnished room. If they are unsure about the finish, a faithful close-up may be a better starting point.

These are alternatives to evaluate, rather than a claim that one format always performs better. The appropriate choice depends on the product and the people buying it.

The operating cost belongs in that comparison too. A product model needs to remain accurate when a range changes. Someone has to check that the selected fabric appears on the right variant and that an older model does not remain attached to the replacement product. Treat those tasks as part of the proposal, rather than maintenance to work out later.

The same discipline applies to a browser-based 3D viewer. Making the feature available without a headset changes how customers reach it, but it still needs a useful job. Being able to rotate a washing machine is only helpful if the view answers something the buyer needs to know.

An experiment may be worthwhile even if it never becomes a main sales channel. What matters is that the team agrees on what it hopes to learn before an attractive demo becomes a commitment to build the whole shop around it.

## What to do about it

### Choose one unanswered product question

This week, review a small set of recent enquiries about one product category. Look for a question that your current page struggles to answer. Record the actual wording rather than turning every request into a general demand for a richer experience.

Write a brief around that question. For example: “Customers need to judge whether this chair leaves enough room beside their sofa.” Then identify the least expensive credible way to help them do that, alongside the immersive option.

If you cannot find a specific question, pause the feature proposal until you have a clearer reason to build it.

### Compare the answer customers reach

Use one product to make a limited prototype. Ask people who might buy it to answer the question using the existing page, then test the proposed treatment with another comparable group where possible. Repeating the task with the same people can help refine a design, but their familiarity will affect the result.

Watch whether they reach a sound answer and what they remain unsure about. Asking whether the experience looks impressive measures something else.

A small test can expose a weak idea or make a promising one more specific. It cannot establish a conversion uplift. Before expanding the work, decide how you would measure its effect in a live trial and what result would justify the ongoing cost.

The case for immersive shopping becomes much clearer when it starts with a customer who needs help choosing something.
