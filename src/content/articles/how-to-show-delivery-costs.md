“Delivery calculated at checkout” gives a customer another job before they know what the purchase will cost.

This guide helps shop owners and designers add useful delivery information to product pages. You will end with an estimate customers can understand and a short check against the amount charged in the basket.

It builds on [our delivery-cost research review](../delivery-cost-product-pages.md) and [Baymard’s annotated shipping examples](https://baymard.com/research-articles/show-shipping-costs-on-product-pages). Those examples are historical research, not claims about the shops’ current pages. We have not run a separate shop audit.

## What to change

### Put a price beside the buying controls

Show the applicable delivery charge or estimate near the purchase button. Baymard’s [2026 product-page review](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-ecommerce-product-page-ux) identifies the missing order estimate as a common gap.

For a hypothetical £24 item with £3.95 delivery, try: “£27.95 including standard delivery to your selected address.” If there are other unavoidable charges, include them before describing the amount as a total.

This example assumes one item. Decide how the message should change when the customer already has something in their basket, then test that case too.

### Explain what the estimate assumes

Display the destination used for the estimate and let customers change it. Baymard’s [Best Buy example](https://baymard.com/research-articles/show-shipping-costs-on-product-pages) illustrates an editable location beside the shipping estimate.

If your rates depend on postcode, a version to test is: “Estimated delivery to BT1: £3.95”, with a “Change postcode” control. The amount here is illustrative.

An unknown destination needs honest wording. Use a supported range if your system cannot quote yet. Avoid displaying the cheapest possible rate as though it applies everywhere.

### Show the charge below the free delivery threshold

Place the normal delivery price beside the threshold. In [Baymard’s Home Depot example](https://baymard.com/research-articles/show-shipping-costs-on-product-pages), having both helped customers assess the offer.

For example: “Standard delivery £3.95. Free on eligible orders of £50 or more.” Replace these figures with your actual terms.

If a £47 basket qualifies only after another £3 of spending, someone can compare that extra purchase with the delivery charge. They need both figures to make the choice.

### Include product-specific surcharges

Account for extra delivery fees on heavy or oversized goods. Baymard’s [World Market example](https://baymard.com/research-articles/show-shipping-costs-on-product-pages) documents the effect of a surcharge on the delivered price.

Take one item with special delivery rules and check the quote your product page produces. If you cannot calculate the fee yet, explain what information is needed. Do not reuse a standard parcel message for a sofa that follows different rules.

## How to check it worked

Spend five minutes on one product first. Use a known delivery address and note the product-page estimate. Add the item to the basket, then enter that same destination at checkout without placing the order.

| Check | Passing result |
| --- | --- |
| One item at the chosen address | The quoted delivery amount matches checkout |
| A different delivery area | The estimate updates or explains its limits |
| Basket below the free delivery threshold | The delivery charge remains visible |
| An eligible basket reaches the threshold | The quote reflects the offer |

If the amounts differ, inspect the delivery rules before adjusting the wording. A clear but inaccurate estimate still gives the customer the wrong answer.

Next, ask someone to tell you the expected total from the product page. Their answer should match the quote for the stated conditions. This is a comprehension check, not evidence of a conversion increase.
