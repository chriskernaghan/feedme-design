When Baymard asked shoppers why they unsubscribe from retailer emails, 48% cited a particular retailer's emails arriving too often, 46% said they get too many newsletters across their inbox, and 44% said their interest in the content had faded. Those are reasons, not separate groups, so you can't add them up, and the survey covers US shoppers describing their own behaviour. It doesn't tell you how often your shop should send anything.

What it does give you is a good reason to look at email from the recipient's side. We went through it in [your shop might be sending too many emails](/articles/your-shop-might-be-sending-too-many-emails/), and these are the ten questions we'd work through before approving the next campaign.

## 1. How many emails does one person actually get?

Your campaign calendar might look sensible while a customer also receives several automated messages that never appear on it. Pick one real journey, such as a new subscriber making a first purchase, and count everything that reaches them.

## 2. Which journeys can one customer be in at the same time?

Picture someone who joins the list for a discount, looks at a product and leaves a basket unfinished. They could qualify for a welcome sequence and a basket reminder while still being eligible for the scheduled promotion. That's a hypothetical, not something Baymard found, so check your own sending records to see whether it happens.

## 3. Does buying something stop the basket reminder?

A reminder that arrives after the order usually means the purchase event isn't ending the sequence. It's one of the most fixable problems on this list, and it's worth checking the event mapping and exclusion rules.

## 4. Do two promotional flows land on the same day?

If they do, decide which one should win for that customer, because the customer won't care which automation was responsible for which message.

## 5. Does one offer undercut another?

A second discount can wreck the deadline on the first. If the first email says the offer ends on Friday and another email offers it again on Sunday, people learn quickly that deadlines don't mean anything.

## 6. What does each message assume about the reader?

Look at the content as well as the timing. A welcome email assumes the person is new, and a basket reminder assumes they haven't bought. When two messages assume different things, one of them is probably wrong.

## 7. Are receipts and delivery updates kept separate from the promotional count?

Keep them in your review, but label their purpose, so you can look at promotional pressure without proposing to cut information someone needs about an order. If a change lowers the count and breaks the receipt, you've solved the wrong problem.

## 8. Would changing the newsletter schedule affect everyone equally?

It wouldn't. For some subscribers the weekly newsletter is the only promotional email they get, while others are also in several automated sequences. Changing the newsletter alone hits both groups without touching the overlap.

## 9. How will you judge a frequency change?

If you test different schedules, give comparable subscribers different promotional rhythms and compare unsubscribes per recipient alongside orders or revenue per recipient over the whole period. Revenue attributed to individual sends can miss what happens across the group.

## 10. What would count as a useful result, and have you agreed it before starting?

Decide the evaluation period and what a meaningful difference looks like in advance. A small or noisy result should leave the question open and shouldn't turn into a claim that one schedule works for every shop.

## Where to start

Take question 1 and follow one journey with a test address, then build a single timeline showing each message and what triggered it. Some sequences run for several days, so your email platform's event and send logs will usually give you a fuller picture than waiting for a fresh test to finish. Once you've got the timeline, you'll probably find a specific collision you can fix before you need an experiment at all.
